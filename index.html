Model 1 — ECM with Nominal Rent Growth

CAPt = β + α₁·CAPt−4 + α₂·T10t + α₃·(Rt/Rt−4)

Q: Why aren't T10 and Rent Growth coefficients equal/opposite? GGF predicts CAP = r − g, so coefficients should be equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. In practice: (1) investors demand a risk premium above T10 that varies with economic conditions, dampening the T10 effect; (2) rent growth data is backward-looking but cap rates reflect expected future growth; (3) cap rates adjust more slowly than the GGF's instantaneous model implies.
Q: Is the long-run T10 coefficient near 1 (as GGF predicts)? Using the ECM long-run formula α₂/(1−α₁), most cities converge near 1.0, supporting GGF theory. San Diego is a notable outlier (1.94) — its very high α₁ (0.96) reflects extremely sticky cap rate adjustment. Owners are reluctant to reprice quickly, but the eventual long-run adjustment is amplified.

Model 2 — ECM with CPI Control

CAPt = β + α₁·CAPt−4 + α₂·T10t + α₃·(Rt/Rt−4) + α₄·(CPIt/CPIt−4)

Q: Why are Rt/Rt-4 coefficients so different between Model 1 and 2? Model 1 bundles real rent growth and inflation together. Model 2 adds CPI to isolate them. With inflation controlled, the pure real rent growth coefficient changes because local market dynamics (supply/demand imbalances) drive real rent changes unevenly across cities — even with constant national inflation, real rent growth varies widely.
Q: Is T10 ≈ average of Rent Growth + CPI coefficients? No — T10 is much smaller while CPI coefficient is large. In the short run, cap rates are more sensitive to inflation changes than to interest rate changes. This makes intuitive sense: interest rate changes are often a delayed response to inflation, so CPI picks up the early signal. Also, the variables are on different scales (ratios vs percentages), making direct magnitude comparison difficult.
